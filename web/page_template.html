A safety check for dates in shared data

Same file. Two readings. One is wrong, and nothing warns you.

dmguard works out whether numeric dates are day-first or month-first by reading the calendar pattern of the whole column. When the data can't prove it, dmguard says so, gives its best suggestion and the reason, and lets you decide.

Why it had to be built

The world writes dates two ways, and software guesses between them

The United States writes the month first. The UK, most of Europe, Latin America, India, Australia and many other countries write the day first. When both numbers are 12 or less, a date such as 03/04/2021 is valid either way.

That is common in real files. Monthly figures are usually dated on the 1st, and every one of them is ambiguous. So are short extracts that only cover the start of a month.

Faced with such a column, the most-used tools pick an order and carry on:

  • pandas assumes month-first unless told otherwise.
  • DuckDB falls back to a fixed preference.
  • Spreadsheets follow the region setting of whatever computer opens the file.

When the guess is wrong, every date moves. Monthly totals land in the wrong months, trends change shape, and deadlines shift. The file still loads and nothing warns.

A file that happens to contain only early-month dates parses at 100% month-first, and every date is silently transposed … Nothing warns, and the trend is wrong rather than absent.Developer bug report, FiBuKI #303

The same failure appears in other bug reports:

  • Submersion #1828: "swaps day and month when the day is 12 or less"
  • BudgetPilot #433: "imports on the wrong date rather than being refused"
  • Tabular #58: "reading 11.01.2018 as 1 November"
  • pandas #12585: inconsistent date parsing

The careful fix used today is to ask the person importing the file. That person often doesn't know the sender's convention either. dmguard answers from the data itself, and still asks when the data can't settle it.

Who it helps, and how it helps people

Anyone who makes decisions from data that crossed a border

Data moves between countries constantly, and a silently shifted date is a quiet way for a decision to go wrong. dmguard is a small, free, offline safeguard: it needs no internet and no accounts, it has no dependencies, and your data never leaves your machine.

Health and humanitarian teams

They combine case counts, stock levels and field reports from many countries. A transposed month moves an outbreak curve or a delivery schedule.

Researchers and students

They reuse open data published in another country's format. A wrong date order changes seasonal patterns and time-series results.

Journalists and fact-checkers

They check government spreadsheets on deadline. They need to know which month a figure belongs to before they publish it.

Small businesses and households

They import bank and accounting exports into budgeting tools. Several of the bug reports above come from exactly these apps.

Software developers

They build "import CSV" features. dmguard gives them a check that refuses to guess, so their users don't inherit the bug.

Everyone downstream

The people affected by a report, a policy or an invoice built on those dates. This benefit is expected, not measured: no study with users has been done yet.

How it works

It reads the calendar, not the locale

The two readings of an ambiguous column are mirror images, value for value, so no single value can separate them. What differs is the pattern the whole column makes on a calendar. dmguard runs these checks in order:

  1. Rule out impossible dates

    If any value would be a 13th month or a 31 April under one reading, that reading is out. Neighbouring date columns in the same file can settle it too.

  2. Measure the calendar pattern

    For each reading, count the bits needed to describe the dates: whether the rows run in order, whether the steps repeat ("+1 month", "+1 day"), and which weekdays appear. Real data is compact under the right reading and scrambled under the wrong one.

  3. Test that it isn't chance

    The difference must be statistically clear, or the column must be more regular than 39 scrambled copies of itself in which day and month are randomly swapped.

  4. Check for a mirror pattern

    "The 1st of every month" read the other way is "1–12 January every year", and both are regular calendars. When the losing reading is also a regular pattern, simplicity is only a preference. Then only weekday evidence can decide, because weekday patterns don't mirror.

  5. Answer, suggest, or refuse

    dmguard reports DMY or MDY with its reasons. Otherwise it reports AMBIGUOUS (with a "likely" order when there is one) and exits with code 2, so an automated pipeline stops rather than loading wrong dates.

  6. Convert without loss

    dmguard fix writes ISO 8601 dates, keeping every digit of the original time. Cells that aren't valid dates stay unchanged and are listed with their line numbers.

Evidence, including what an outside review found

No silent errors, including on patterns built to fool it

Version 1.0 was tested on 22 date columns from public datasets (exchange rates, COVID-19 counts, consumer prices, stock prices, weather, employment). The dates and their order are real; we wrote each column both ways and cut shorter extracts. It answered 72.4% of the ambiguous cases with no wrong answers.

An independent reviewer then found four real defects:

  • it copied one column's convention onto another column;
  • it dropped fractional seconds;
  • it reported success despite invalid cells;
  • it confidently misread US-format "1–12 January" data as "the 1st of every month".

The last one mattered most: every automatic answer in the 1.0 test came from exactly that kind of mirror pattern. Version 1.1 fixes all four and was re-tested, including on a fresh set of 840 deliberately misleading columns.

Everything not answered was flagged for a person, never loaded wrong. On the 1,962 real cases whose dates were not ambiguous, every version was 100% correct. A 100,000-row column takes at most 1.3 seconds. Full tables are in RESULTS.md, and the review fixes are in SPEC_v3_REVIEW_FIXES.md.

What this means, plainly

  • By default, dmguard 1.1 almost never answers on its own for real monthly data. It flags the column and suggests an order, and on the real data its suggestion was right in all 902 cases where it made one. On patterns built to mislead it, the suggestions were right 70% of the time. That is why they are only suggestions.
  • It proves the order outright when the pattern has no mirror image, for example weekly data or monthly data dated on the first trading day (weekdays only), given enough rows.
  • One goal set before the first test, answering at least half of the ambiguous real cases automatically, is not met by 1.1's default. Safety was chosen over coverage.
  • The real-data test was no longer unseen when 1.1 was re-tested, and the misleading-pattern set is synthetic. No people have used the tool yet.
  • Novelty: no close equivalent was found in the documented search as of 29 September 2026. Date-format detection itself, including compression-based methods, already exists; what's new here is using calendar structure to choose the order, together with the mirror-pattern check and refusing to guess.
Where to get it and test it yourself

Free, open source, and runs anywhere Python 3.9+ runs

The source code, tests, benchmark and full research log are public on GitHub under the MIT licence. dmguard needs nothing beyond Python itself.

Install and use

pip install "git+https://github.com/arcofcreations-collab/Journeymandigital@main"
dmguard check your_file.csv
dmguard fix your_file.csv -o clean.csv

check reports each date column and why. fix writes a copy with dates in ISO 8601 (2019-02-01). Add --assume DMY or --assume MDY to record your own decision for columns the data can't settle.
